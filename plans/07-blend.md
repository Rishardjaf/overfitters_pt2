# Plan 07 — Blend

**Role:** combine the members from plans 01–06 into the submitted prediction. Usually the
cheapest remaining gain once two or more models exist.

**Status:** not run. Requires at least two completed member plans with retained out-of-fold
predictions.

---

## 1. Purpose

Averaging models with imperfectly correlated errors reduces variance without adding bias. On
tabular competitions a simple blend of two or three decent models routinely beats the best
single model by 0.05–0.15 RMSE, for a fraction of the effort that tuning the best member
further would cost.

Given **F7** — the signal is weak, so every individual model is fitting a modest amount of real
structure plus a lot of noise — averaging is particularly well motivated here. The noise
components partially cancel; the shared signal does not.

## 2. What the members must supply

This plan has no data cleaning of its own. Its inputs are model outputs, and the integrity
problems are correspondingly different — alignment rather than imputation.

**Identical folds.** Every member's out-of-fold predictions must come from the *same* fold
definition, generated by the same validation code. Two members validated on slightly different
splits cannot be blended honestly, and the resulting weights would be fitted on a mismatch.

**Stable row alignment.** Out-of-fold predictions must be keyed by the row identifier, never by
positional index. The shared preparation layer reindexes onto an hourly grid, inserts padding
rows and filters them out again; any member that stores predictions positionally will silently
misalign against a member that stores them keyed. **This is the leading source of a blend that
scores worse than its best member for no visible reason.**

**A common unit.** Members may train on Δ or on the raw level depending on how plan 01's gate
resolves. All predictions must be reconstructed to the same units — predicted next-hour
concentration — *before* blending. Blending a Δ prediction with a level prediction is a
type error that will not raise one.

**The missing-anchor rows.** The 371 test rows from **P1** are reconstructed by whatever
mechanism plan 01 settles on. Every member must use the *same* mechanism, or the blend averages
predictions built on different anchors and the disagreement will be mistaken for model
diversity.

**Retained from the first run.** Storing out-of-fold predictions is cheap at generation time
and expensive to recreate later. Every member plan should write them out on its first
execution, not when this plan starts.

## 3. Weighting approach

**Start with the unweighted mean.** It fits nothing, so it cannot overfit, and it is the
honest reference that any weighted scheme must beat. Report it always.

**Then non-negative constrained weights summing to one.** Negative weights are technically
optimal in-sample and almost always an overfitting artefact on a handful of folds — constrain
them out.

**The weight-fitting itself needs validation.** This is the subtle problem in this plan. Fitting
weights on all out-of-fold predictions and then reporting the resulting score reuses the same
data twice and produces an optimistic number. With five or six folds and up to six members, the
weight vector has enough freedom to fit fold-level noise.

Two acceptable protocols:

- Fit the weights on the earlier folds and evaluate them on the latest fold only. Consistent
  with the chronological structure, and the latest fold most resembles the test period.
- Nest the weight fitting inside each fold. More correct, more machinery.

The first is recommended on effort grounds, with the caveat that it leaves a single fold's worth
of evidence for the weights and should therefore be compared against the unweighted mean rather
than assumed better.

**Prefer few members.** Two or three well-decorrelated models generally blend better than six
mediocre ones, and every additional member adds a weight to fit. Use the residual correlations
reported by plans 03, 04 and 05 to select, not the standalone RMSEs.

## 4. Member selection

Selection is on **decorrelation**, not individual score. A slightly worse model with distinct
errors contributes more than a marginally better one that agrees with plan 01 everywhere.

Expected ordering by usefulness to the blend, to be confirmed by the measured residual
correlations:

| Member | Expected contribution | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Plan 01 — LightGBM | Anchor member, highest weight | Strongest expected standalone |
| Plan 02 — CatBoost | High, if its categorical handling differs meaningfully | Different encoding of `station` × `wd` |
| Plan 04 — Ridge / ElasticNet | **Potentially highest per unit of standalone score** | Only non-tree member; fails smoothly rather than by flattening at the training boundary |
| Plan 05 — RandomForest / ExtraTrees | Moderate | Only bagged member, but attenuates the spike rows that carry 77% of squared error (**F10**) |
| Plan 03 — XGBoost | Lowest, most likely to be redundant | Same family and growth paradigm as plan 01 |
| Plan 06 — Two-stage spike | Unknown | Different enough to be valuable, but its calibration risk propagates into the blend |

## 5. Validation

As specified in `plans/README.md`. Additionally:

- Report the unweighted mean and the fitted-weight blend side by side, always.
- Report the **spike-vs-calm breakdown** for the blend. Averaging shrinks toward the mean, and
  **F10** says 6.78% of rows carry 77.0% of the squared error. A blend can improve the headline
  while flattening exactly those rows. Check rather than assume.
- Report the pairwise residual correlation matrix across members. It explains the weights and
  is the main artefact for review.

## 6. Success criteria

- Beats the best single member on the heating-season folds by more than 0.05 RMSE, reproducing
  across folds.
- Does not degrade event-row RMSE relative to the best single member.
- The fitted-weight version beats the unweighted mean. **If it does not, ship the unweighted
  mean** — that is a legitimate outcome, not a failure, and it is more robust to the test
  distribution shift (**F8**).

## 7. Risks and failure modes

**Row misalignment** (section 2). Silent, and it degrades the blend below its best member.
Key everything by identifier.

**Overfitted weights** on few folds and many members. Mitigated by the constraint, the protocol
in section 3, and preferring few members.

**Unit mismatch** between Δ-target and level-target members (section 2).

**Spike flattening.** Averaging is smoothing, and smoothing is the wrong direction for 77% of
the squared error.

**Correlated members giving false confidence.** Six models that agree are not six pieces of
evidence. The residual correlation matrix is what distinguishes genuine diversity from an
echo chamber.

## 8. Recommendation

Run this **as soon as two members exist** — plans 01 and 02 are enough. Do not wait for all six.
An early two-model blend establishes the harness, exposes the alignment bugs while there are
only two things to reconcile, and produces a submittable artefact early. Additional members can
then be added incrementally, each evaluated on whether it improves the blend rather than on its
own score.

## 9. Effort estimate

Low, provided members retained their out-of-fold predictions from their first run. Most of the
cost is reconciling alignment and units — which is exactly why running it early, with two
members, is cheaper than running it late with six.
